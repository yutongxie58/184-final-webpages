Underwater Volumetric Scattering

Summary

We're extending our HW3 path tracer to render underwater scenes with volumetric scattering. Instead of light only bouncing off surfaces, rays will now scatter within the water itself, letting us capture depth-dependent haze and light shafts. We'll start with uniform water density, then extend to spatially-varying density fields that simulate drifting particle clouds and changing turbidity.

Problem Description

Our HW3 path tracer only knows how to handle light at surfaces. When a ray hits an object, we compute reflection, refraction, or diffuse scattering—but between surfaces, we assume light just travels through empty space without any interaction. This works fine for rendering solid objects in air, but it completely breaks down for scenes with fog, smoke, or water, where the medium itself affects how light travels.

Underwater scenes are a perfect example of where this limitation shows up. In real water, there are tons of suspended particles that absorb and scatter light as it travels. Objects farther away fade into murky blue-green haze. Light from the surface creates visible shafts as it scatters through the water. Visibility drops dramatically with depth. None of this happens in our current renderer because we're missing the entire concept of participating media.

The main technical challenge is figuring out what happens along each ray segment. Before, we just marched rays until they hit a surface. Now, we need to account for the possibility that a ray scatters somewhere in the middle of the water before it ever reaches an object. We need to sample where (or if) scattering happens, compute how much light gets absorbed along the way, and handle lighting at these medium scattering events.

Here's our plan: we'll model water as a participating medium with absorption and scattering coefficients (σ_a and σ_s). For each ray, we sample a scattering distance from an exponential distribution based on these coefficients. If that distance is shorter than our surface intersection, the ray scatters in the medium instead. When scattering happens, we evaluate direct lighting by shooting shadow rays to lights (accounting for transmittance through the water) and then sample a new direction to continue the path. For transmittance, we'll use Beer-Lambert law, which gives us that exponential visibility falloff with distance.

We'll start with uniform density water and isotropic scattering to get the basics working. Once we can render basic underwater haze and light shafts, we want to push it further with spatially-varying density fields. Instead of constant density everywhere, we'd use a 2D density function (likely procedural noise or a height-based field) where particle concentration varies across the scene. This requires ray marching to integrate transmittance through varying density, which is more expensive, but it lets us create localized particle clouds, patches of murky water, and more visually interesting underwater environments. It's a natural extension once the baseline volumetric rendering is in place.

Goals and Deliverables

What We Plan to Deliver

Our baseline goal is to implement volumetric scattering in a homogeneous underwater medium. This means extending our HW3 path tracer to handle light interactions within the medium itself, not just at surfaces.

Core features we will implement:

  • Medium representation: A Medium class with constant absorption and scattering coefficients (σ_a, σ_s) that defines the water's optical properties.
  • Transmittance computation: Beer-Lambert attenuation along all ray segments (both camera rays and shadow rays) to model exponential visibility falloff.
  • Free-flight sampling: Sample scattering distances from an exponential distribution to determine whether rays scatter in the medium or reach surfaces.
  • Medium scattering events: Evaluate direct lighting at scattering points using shadow rays with transmittance, and sample new directions using an isotropic phase function.
  • Directional light source: Add overhead directional lighting to simulate sunlight entering the water and creating light shafts.

Images we will produce:

  • Side-by-side comparison of the same underwater scene rendered with and without volumetric scattering (showing the difference participating media makes)
  • Parameter sweep showing how different scattering coefficients affect visibility— from clear water to murky, turbid conditions
  • Depth comparison showing objects at different distances fading into blue-green haze
  • Light shaft visualization with directional lighting from above creating visible god rays through the water
  • Convergence study comparing renders at 64, 256, 1024 samples per pixel to show noise reduction and performance characteristics

Quality metrics:

  • Visual correctness: Does the haze increase with depth? Do light shafts appear where we expect them? Does transmittance follow Beer-Lambert law?
  • Performance: Render time comparison between baseline path tracer and volumetric version (we expect 2-3x slowdown due to medium sampling)
  • Convergence rate: How many samples are needed for clean images? We'll plot MSE vs sample count to quantify noise reduction.

Questions we'll answer:

  • How do absorption vs scattering coefficients affect the visual appearance?
  • What's the performance cost of adding volumetric scattering?
  • How many samples per pixel do we need for acceptable image quality?

What We Hope to Deliver

If the baseline is completed ahead of schedule, we'll extend to spatially-varying density fields. This would replace our constant coefficients with a 2D density function that varies across the scene.

Stretch features:

  • Heterogeneous density: Use procedural noise (Perlin or simplex) or a 2D texture map to define spatially-varying particle concentration. This requires ray marching to integrate transmittance through varying density.
  • Localized particle clouds: Create patches of murky water or drifting sediment clouds that affect visibility locally.
  • Animated density: Evolve the density field over time using simple advection or animated noise to simulate moving particles.

Additional images if we reach stretch goals:

  • Renders showing localized particle clouds with varying turbidity across the scene
  • Animation frames showing density fields evolving over time
  • Comparison between homogeneous and heterogeneous media in the same scene

We're confident about the baseline because it's a well-understood extension of path tracing with clear references (PBRT Chapter 11, 15). The stretch goal is more ambitious but builds naturally on the baseline framework—it's mainly about replacing constant lookups with density field queries and adding ray marching.
